1The price gap is real. The accuracy gap is not.

Across all 2,000 prompts the frontier tier costs a mean 62.6x more per turn, yet buys a mean accuracy advantage of only +0.018 on a 0–1 correctness scale. The distribution is starker than the mean: the cheapest model was already perfect on 70% of prompts, the two tiers tied on 84%, and the frontier model lost outright on 6.8%.

2The 62x pays off in exactly one place: the code-writing job.

Grouping each prompt by the job it asks the model to do, the frontier gain over the cheap gpt-4o-mini is concentrated almost entirely on the profiler and coder. On the planner — the largest prompt in the set and the most expensive job to upgrade — it is zero. This agrees with published guidance on routing agent traffic: spend on the coder, leave the planner on the cheap model.

Frontier advantage by task domain

Multitask knowledge and math reasoning gain; graduate science QA and multi-step deduction lose. A 29-point swing — no single global route setting captures it. Scores are graded 0–1, so a loss is usually a wrong final value, not an inability to do the task.

3The biggest routing decision is “no fallback needed”.

Grouping by the fallback policy recorded for each prompt, the largest segment is the literal string None — 1,631 rows, 81.6% — where the cheap model already sufficed. That word is a category here, not a missing value: read carelessly it would delete four-fifths of the dataset. Read correctly, it is the single largest route in the system.

4A router is learnable from pre-call features alone.

Using only what is known before a call runs — prompt structure, domain, job type, reference length — a model predicts high_compute_route at 0.956, against 0.500 for guessing. It beats the trivial baseline on the confidence interval and is certified deployable.
